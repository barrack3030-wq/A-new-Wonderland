Many visitors come to the Banggai Islands for lakes, waterfalls and beaches. Fewer know that the archipelago gives its name to a small fish that aquarium keepers around the world recognise at a glance: the **Banggai cardinalfish** (*Pterapogon kauderni*). If you plan a **Banggai trip** that includes snorkeling, this fish is the one animal worth learning about before you get in the water.

## What the fish looks like

The Banggai cardinalfish is small, reaching about 8 cm in length. It has a silvery body, bold black vertical bars, white spots and long, trailing fins. It was formally described by the Dutch ichthyologist Koumans in 1933. Because of its looks it became popular in the marine aquarium trade, which is the main reason it is now a conservation concern.

## Where it lives

The species is endemic to the Banggai Islands in Central Sulawesi, meaning it occurs naturally nowhere else. Reference sources also record a small population in the harbour of Luwuk on the mainland coast. Populations found elsewhere, for example in Lembeh Strait in North Sulawesi and at Secret Bay in Bali, are generally described as the result of introductions.

It favours calm, shallow water: sheltered reefs, seagrass beds and sandy areas, most often at around 1.5 to 2.5 m depth. It is commonly found near long-spined sea urchins and seagrass, and juveniles shelter among anemones. For a snorkeler this is useful to know, because you do not need to dive deep to see it, and you do not need to touch anything to find it.

## An unusual way of raising young

The Banggai cardinalfish is a paternal mouthbrooder. The male carries the fertilised eggs, up to roughly 90, in his mouth and keeps them there for about a month. The young hatch as fully formed miniatures and stay close to the adults instead of drifting away as plankton. This helps explain two things: the fish tends to stay in small groups close to where it was born, and a population that is heavily fished does not easily recover from far away.

## Conservation status

The species is listed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List. The main documented pressure has been collection for the aquarium trade. Published accounts describe exports in the range of several hundred thousand fish per year in the early 2000s, and steep local declines, including a population that disappeared entirely. Captive breeding has since taken over a large part of the market.

According to a Dialogue Earth report, the fish is now protected under an Indonesian ministerial decree, and regulated collection is limited to periods outside the main spawning peaks. The same report says that catching for export around the Banggai Islands themselves has largely stopped, with the current national quota drawn mainly from introduced populations elsewhere. Rules and quotas change, so treat this as background and confirm the current position with local authorities if you need it.

## How to watch it responsibly

- **Look, do not touch.** Keep your hands, fins and equipment away from the fish and from the sea urchins and seagrass it uses as shelter.
- **Never chase or herd the fish** for a photo. Hover at a distance and let the group move as it likes.
- **Do not buy live marine animals** as souvenirs, and do not accept an offer to catch one for you.
- **Stay off the bottom.** In shallow water it is easy to kick seagrass or crush coral without noticing. Float horizontally and keep your fins up.
- **Do not feed the fish.**
- **Ask your guide** which areas are suitable and whether any local rules apply on the day.

No operator can promise a sighting. Visibility, tide, weather and the exact site all affect what you will see. If you want to see the fish, make that known when planning so the boat and snorkeling stops can be chosen with it in mind.
